### Label taxonomy (Label › Sublabel — description; examples)

* Grammar

  General Guideline (Multilingual Use):
  Apply sublabels based on function, not strict part-of-speech definitions from English.
  Do not force categories that are not expressed in a given language.
  When a grammatical function is expressed differently (e.g., via particles, affixes, or word order), choose the closest functional category.
  Use Grammar sublabels when the error affects sentence structure or grammatical relationships; use Spelling sublabels when the error affects only the written form of a word.

  * Adjective — description: marks an error where an adjective is missing, incorrect, or unnecessarily added. Such issues affect how nouns are described or qualified. A missing adjective occurs when a needed descriptor is omitted. A replacement adjective appears when the wrong adjective is used. An unnecessary adjective appears when an extra or irrelevant adjective is present.; examples: "The movie we saw was very {bored=>boring}."; "She wore a {red =>}red dress."; "I was 27 years {=>old }then."

  * Adverb — description: marks an error involving a missing, incorrect, or unnecessary adverb. These mistakes affect how verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs describe the manner, degree, frequency, or time of an action.; examples: "We worked {good=>well} together but did not finish on time."; "Please drive {=>carefully }on the icy road."; "The exam was {very =>}extremely difficult."

  * Agreement — description: used when words that should agree in grammatical form (such as number, person, or gender) do not match. Apply this label when the disagreement occurs between nouns and determiners, adjectives, or pronouns. For subject–verb agreement, use the dedicated sublabel instead.; examples: "It only takes a few {minute=>minutes} for Tom to walk home from work."; "Every human has {its=>their} own political will."; "Besides, {this=>these} games are open to all."

  * Conjunction — description: marks an error involving a missing, incorrect, or unnecessary conjunction (e.g., and, but, or, because, although). These issues affect how ideas, clauses, or phrases are connected logically. A missing conjunction occurs when a connector is omitted. A replacement conjunction occurs when the wrong connector is used. An unnecessary conjunction appears when an extra one causes confusion.; examples: "It's not cold, {neither=>nor} hot."; "My office days are Monday {=>and }Tuesday."; "It's a little {and =>}green lamp!".

  * Determiner — description: marks an error with determiners — words placed before nouns to clarify reference (e.g., *a*, *the*, *this*, *these*, *both*). Some determiners work only with singular or plural nouns, and they should not be used with personal pronouns.; examples: "To deal with such {those =>}issues, users are required to know the troubleshooting for different errors."; "He lost {a=>the} key to his room."; "She bought {=>a }cat."

  * Morphology — description: relates to choosing the correct word form within a word family (e.g., *choose, choice, chosen, choosable*). Words from the same family may differ by part of speech but share related meanings. The correct choice depends on the sentence context.; examples: "Improper {manage=>management} of these issues will create more suffering in the affected area."; "That happened {quick=>quickly}."

  * Negation — description: marks a problem with how negation is expressed or omitted. This includes missing or extra negative words, or incorrect forms that make a statement unintentionally positive, negative, or confusing.; examples: "There is almost {none=>no} related research study available online."; "It's {=>no }longer a secret."

  * Noun — description: marks an error where a noun is missing, incorrect, or unnecessary, affecting the meaning of the sentence. This includes using the wrong noun, omitting a needed one, or adding an extra noun. Agreement issues are not included here.; examples: "I found the ball in my {brothers room=>brother's room}."; "I strive to be open and mindful of the ways others might receive {fee=>feedback} as well."

  * Preposition — description: used when a preposition is missing, incorrect, or unnecessary, affecting grammatical accuracy. This includes incorrect prepositions in phrases such as *depend on*, *listen to*, or *interested in* after verbs, nouns, or adjectives.; examples: "The library is {in=>on} the north side of the quad."; "I am waiting {=>for }your reply."; "Here the engineers can examine {about =>}the bug."

  * Pronoun — description: marks an error involving a missing, incorrect, or unnecessary pronoun, leading to unclear or incorrect reference. For number or gender mismatches, use the Agreement sublabel instead.; examples: "Did you find {you=>your} keys yet?"; "However, {=>it }also contains blood vessels."; "My car {it =>}is very new."

  * Verb — description: marks an error where a verb is wrong, missing, or unnecessary, excluding tense or subject–verb agreement issues. This includes incorrect lexical choice, missing auxiliary verbs, or extra verbs that make the sentence ungrammatical or awkward. Also use this sublabel when "to" is inserted or removed before a base verb to correctly form the infinitive.; examples: "I've already {payed=>paid} for the shipment."; "I {did=>made} some mistakes."; "If I {will =>}do this, I will have to thoroughly prepare, I thought."; "I {=>look }forward to hearing from you!"; "The internship experiences helped me {to =>}develop my interpersonal skills."

  * VerbSVA — description: used when the subject and verb do not agree in number or person. A singular subject must take a singular verb, and a plural subject must take a plural verb.; examples: "Many people {wants=>want} to travel during the summer."; "One lantern {glow=>glows} beside the hatch."

  * VerbTenseForm — description: applies when the verb form or tense is incorrect. Verbs conjugate by person, number, tense, and mood; the correct form depends on context, auxiliary verbs, or modals.; examples: "It's really going to {depended=>depend} on you."; "Your tuition could be completely {cover=>covered}."; "I don't recall {to meet=>meeting} you before."; "We will be in touch as soon as the update {will be=>is} ready."; "We all knew she {has=>had} been crying ."

  * Particle — description: marks an error involving a missing, incorrect, or unnecessary particle. Particles are short functional words used with verbs or clauses to modify or complete their meaning (e.g., in phrasal verbs, aspect marking, or grammatical relations). Errors occur when the wrong particle is used, a required particle is omitted, or an extra particle is added.; examples: "Please fill {in=>out} this form."; "The plane took {off =>}off on time."; "Let's sort this {=>out }later."
  Clarification (Cross-linguistic use):
  In English, particles typically form part of phrasal verbs (e.g., give up, take off).
  In other languages, particles may mark grammatical roles, aspect, mood, or emphasis.
  Use this sublabel for short functional words that modify or complete the meaning of a verb or clause.
  Use Particle when the word forms part of the verb's meaning; use Preposition when it introduces a complement or object.
  Examples:
  English (phrasal verb particle): "Please fill {in=>out} this form."
  German (separable particle): "Ich stehe um 7 Uhr {=>auf}."
  Japanese (case particle): "水{が=>を}飲む。"
  Chinese (aspect particle): "他到{=>了 }学校。"
  Polish (modal/emphasis particle): "Czy on {=>by }przyszedł?"

* Punctuation

  * ClosingPunctuation — description: used when the punctuation mark at the end of a sentence (e.g., ., ?, !) is missing, incorrect, or unnecessary. It applies when the sentence should end differently or includes an extra mark. This category focuses on sentence-ending punctuation only, not internal punctuation like commas or semicolons.; examples: "Would you like to carpool{.=>?}"; "She locked the door{=>.}"; "I'm sorry for being late{..=>.}"

  * SentenceSplit — description: used when two or more independent clauses or sentences are incorrectly connected or separated. This includes run-on sentences, comma splices, and unclear sentence boundaries. For changes that only replace one sentence-ending punctuation mark with another, use the ClosingPunctuation label instead.; examples: "The kiln cooled{=>;} the glaze hardened."; "No one knew where he came from{, he=>; he} just appeared out of thin air."; "The trail stayed closed{. Because=> because} the bridge had shifted."; "I enjoy running in the morning{.=>, which} helps me clear my mind."

  * Other — description: used for punctuation issues that don't fit other categories, such as comma insertion/removal or confusion between semicolons and commas.; examples: "We packed a flashlight, a roll of tape{=>,} and a bag of clips."; "This winter was colder{,=>} than the past five winters."; "Prior to the scandal{;=>,} the governor's approval rating was high."

* Spelling

  * SpellConfused_Lowercase — description: marks an error where a word is capitalized incorrectly and should appear in lowercase. This usually happens with common nouns, internal sentence words, or function words.; examples: "Yes, {This=>this} suite is good for one person only."

  * SpellConfused_Capitalize — description: used when a word's capitalization is incorrect and should be changed to match meaning or convention (e.g., `mercury` for the element vs. `Mercury` for the planet); examples: "{thank=>Thank} you for the information."; "We usually shop on {saturday=>Saturday}."

  * SpellConfused_Confused — description: used when a word is correctly spelled but confused with another valid word that has a small edit distance (e.g., form vs. from, affect vs. effect). These mistakes typically result from typing or cognitive slips rather than true misspellings, and both options exist as valid dictionary words. This also includes common apostrophe errors where one valid form is confused with another.; examples: "The stone sank to the {flour=>floor} of the tank."; "A {dear=>deer} stepped onto the trail."; "They can wear black or blue trousers with a new {withe=>white} T-shirt."; "The drummer rested his {base=>bass} drum on the mat."; "The owl cleaned {it's=>its} wings."

  * Misspelling — description: used for words that contain spelling mistakes.; examples: "We {recive dyour=>received your} inquiry on Monday."; "{Im=>I'm} fine"; "The {lanten=>lantern} cast a {shdaow=>shadow} on the wall."

  * Merge — description: marks cases where two words should be combined into one.; examples: "This is both touching and {heart breaking=>heartbreaking}."; "We're looking for a {dog friendly=>dog-friendly} hotel."

  * Diacritic — description: marks an error involving missing, incorrect, or unnecessary diacritical marks (e.g., accents, tildes, umlauts, cedillas). Use this sublabel when the base letters are correct but diacritics are used incorrectly or omitted.; examples: "I visited a {cafe=>café} in Paris."; "El {nino=>niño} está jugando."; "Das ist {uber=>über} wichtig."; "Zrobiłem {lazienke=>łazienkę}."
  Clarification (Cross-linguistic use):
  In many languages (e.g., Spanish, German, Polish), diacritics are required for correct spelling and may change meaning.
  In English, diacritics are often optional or stylistic (e.g., cafe vs café); use this sublabel only when diacritics are required by context, convention, or proper nouns.
  Use Spelling › Other when diacritic errors occur together with other spelling issues.

  * Other — description: covers other spelling-related issues not included above, such as incomplete words or letter repetitions. Also use this sublabel when a single annotation includes multiple types of spelling issues (e.g., casing + misspelling), for one-word corrections that complete or fix a truncated or incorrectly formed word, and when a correction involves splitting a hyphenated word into two or more words for sentence correctness. Do not use this sublabel when an informal but correctly spelled word is replaced with a more formal alternative — use Rephrasing › Formality instead.; examples: "The stagehand lit the {lan=>lantern} before the show."; "It {wouuuuuld=>would} {beee=>be} nice if we could go outside."; "The painter rinsed the {BRUH=>brush} in the sink."; "A {snaaaiiil=>snail} crawled across the pavement."; "Count on {u=>us} for quality, availability, and professional service."; "The dog slept {in-front=>in front} of the fireplace."; "We stopped for a snack {on-the-way=>on the way} home."; "{Psychological-development=>Psychological development} disorders were further analyzed by specific diagnosis."

* Formatting

  * DateFormat — description: ensures dates follow consistent formatting standards (month-day order, abbreviations, leading zeros).; examples: "{Sep 4=>September 04}"; "Applications are due {July 10=>10 July}"

  * TimeFormat — description: standardizes how time expressions are written (e.g., using periods in *a.m.*, proper spacing, or 12-hour vs. 24-hour notation).; examples: "I am available any day from 8:00 {am=>a.m.} to 12{noon=>:00 noon}"

  * AbbreviationFormat — description: standardizes abbreviations by adjusting punctuation or capitalization.; examples: "{N.A.S.A.=>NASA}"; "The map legend uses {ave=>Ave.} for every avenue."

  * CurrencyFormat — description: used when a currency amount is formatted incorrectly, such as using the wrong symbol, code, or placement. This includes wrong symbols (e.g., `$` instead of `£`), incorrect symbol placement, or inconsistent decimal/grouping formats.; examples: "{120$=>$120}"; "In the UK, the price of this game was {€=>£}20."

  * MeasurementFormat — description: used when units of measurement or scientific notation are formatted incorrectly. This includes issues with symbol placement, or the use of superscripts, subscripts, and degree signs. The sublabel applies only to formatting, not to the numeric values themselves.; examples: "7.5 {m/s2=>m/s²}"; "The water was heated to {100C=>100 °C}."

  * RedundantSpace — description: used when unnecessary spaces appear and disrupt text flow or formatting.; examples: "No{  => }one cared."

  * MissingSpace — description: marks missing spaces between words, punctuation, or other elements that need separation.; examples: "I have warned you,{=> }and I will do it again."

  * SpellOutNumber — description: applies when numbers should be written as words according to style or formatting rules.; examples: "{10=>Ten} black cats were sitting on the roof."; "{4th=>fourth} and {5th=>fifth} pages"

  * Other — description: used for any other formatting-related corrections, e.g., citation styles or quotation mark consistency.; examples: "An earlier study reports the same pattern (Author A {&=>and} Author B 2006, 99–100)."; "The catalog called it {‘moon clay”=>“moon clay”} in every heading."

* SentenceStructure

  * WordOrder — description: used when the sequence of words in a phrase or sentence is incorrect, making it ungrammatical or unclear. Word order changes can also alter meaning.; examples: "This place isn't {enough big=>big enough} for the two of us."

  * SentenceFragment — description: marks a sentence missing a necessary subject or verb, resulting in an incomplete thought. If the subject is merely rephrased to be more or less wordy, use the Rephrasing label instead; if the change also affects grammatical correctness, use Other/Compound.; examples: "{Depends=>It depends} on how you look at it."; "I hope {=>you }are well."

* Other

  Other › Compound — definition
  Use this sublabel when a single correction covers multiple error types or does not belong to a specific category but is nonetheless required for correctness.
  If an annotation includes only changes that fall within one specific category and subcategory, use that specific label instead of Compound — even if multiple edits occur.
  If multiple edits are made but all belong to the same category and subcategory, classify the annotation under that specific sublabel rather than Compound.
  Count error types, not the number of mechanical edits. A single replacement that corrects multiple issues within the same subcategory (e.g., spelling and capitalization within Spelling › Other) remains in that subcategory.
  Examples:
   "She bought {=>a }cat." → Grammar › Determiner
   "{Kitchen=>The kitchen} door sticks in winter." → Grammar › Determiner
   "I found {TEH BRUSHH=>the brush} behind the easel." → Spelling › Other
  Use Other › Compound when the annotation includes changes related to more than one category or subcategory.
   Examples:
   "For the border, choose {=>a narrow }brush instead of the broad one." (Determiner + Adjective)
   "We repaired {bird house=>a birdhouse} before spring." (Determiner + Spelling › Merge)
   "Each project needs to be followed up {the=>on its} progress." (Preposition + Pronoun insertion)

  Granular classification
  Classify Compound annotations by complexity to ensure consistency.
  Clarification (Counting Changes)
  A "change" refers to a distinct error type, not the number of character-level edits.
  Insertions, deletions, and replacements are counted based on the grammatical or formatting category affected.
  Multiple character corrections within the same subcategory count as one change.
  If multiple edits all belong to the same category and subcategory, do not use Compound.
  Examples:
  Determiner insertion + Adjective insertion → 2 changes
  Spelling correction affecting multiple letters → 1 change (Spelling)
  Clarification (Formatting and Word Boundaries)
  Count MissingSpace or RedundantSpace as a separate change only when spacing is independently incorrect in the original text and is corrected in the rewrite.
  If spacing adjusts solely as a result of inserting or deleting a word (i.e., forming correct word boundaries), do not count it as a separate Formatting change.
  Examples:
   "We hung {paperlanterns above arch=>paper lanterns above the arch}."
   (MissingSpace + Determiner → Compound_Trivial)
  "I like {=>that }turtle."
   (Determiner insertion only → Grammar › Determiner)
  "No{  => }one cared."
   (Formatting › RedundantSpace)
  Clarification (Lowercasing and Structural Insertions)
  Do not count lowercasing as a separate change when a word becomes non-initial due to inserting or replacing words at the beginning of a sentence.
  Count lowercasing only when the original casing is incorrect.
  Examples:
  "After the rain{ We=>, we} opened the shed."
  (SpellConfused_Lowercase + Punctuation insertion → Compound_Trivial)
  "{We=>Are we} gonna do it?"
  (Verb insertion only → Grammar › Verb)
  "Good {Morning=>morning}, Sir,"
  (Spelling › SpellConfused_Lowercase)

  * Compound_Trivial — This level applies only when the annotation already qualifies as Other › Compound (i.e., involves more than one category or subcategory). Use when the annotation includes exactly two changes, both belonging to two of the following categories:
  Incorrectly spelled word; examples: "यह {कितब=>किताब} मेज़ पर रख दो।"; "The duckling flapped its {wigns=>wings}."; "We hung the tools on a {pegbaord=>pegboard}."
  Capitalization/lowercasing; examples: "The library reopens on {monday=>Monday}."; "Good {Morning=>morning,} Sir,"
  SpellConfused_Confused with character edit distance = 1; example: "We spread jam on the {coast=>toast}."
  Punctuation; examples: "{maybe=>Maybe,} but I doubt it will happen {soOn=>soon.}"; "Find the washer{.=>!}"; "VGA ports are similar to serial port connectors{=>; the} only difference is, that serial connector has pins and VGA has holes."
  Preposition insertion, deletion, or replacement with another preposition; examples: "May I check on the status {for=>of the} agreement?"; "Progress {=>over the }last 27 days"; "We had a {full time of=>full-time} job."
  Particle insertion, deletion, or replacement with another particle; example: "Please note I will be travelling for the next day and may not be able to reply right{=> away.}"
  Determiner insertion, deletion, or replacement with another determiner; examples: "{given=>Given a} list of colors, sort them by name."; "The parser returned {html=>an HTML} document."; "I want to prepare {a Interview=>an interview} assessment document."
  Formatting (only MissingSpace or RedundantSpace); examples: "{5)What did=>5) What did the} owl find?"; "Slide the tray{  in=> to the} left."
  One verb form is replaced with another verb form (without changing the root (the basic core of a word) and part of speech); example: "We went {skate=>skating,} and the rink stayed open late."
  Verb tense; example: "There are situations where she mistakenly did something, but instead of blaming others or running from it, she was honest and {find=>found a} solution to it"
  VerbSVA; examples: "{i likes=>I like} turtles."; "Therefore, {household needs =>households need }intervention such as skills training or seed capital for livelihood to sustain their level of well-being."
  An adjective is replaced with the same adjective in a different degree of comparison (e.g., positive → comparative, comparative → superlative); example: "It was {best thn=>better than} I thought"
  One noun form is replaced with another noun form (without changing the root or part of speech); examples: "Besides, {these games=>this game} is open to all."; "Before I went on the stage, my {emotion was=>emotions were} truly complicated, with a mix of nervousness and reluctance"; "{happy new years=>Happy New Year}"; "The three {turtle=>turtles'} shared tank needed fresh water."

  * Compound_Moderate — Use when the annotation includes: Two or more changes from Moderate categories below, or at least one Moderate change combined with at least one Trivial change, or three or more changes from Trivial categories.
  Moderate categories:
  SpellConfused_Confused with character edit distance > 1; example: "We reduced {waist in workshop=>waste in the workshop} by reusing clay scraps."
  Deletion of 2+ words. It should apply only when the deletion spans multiple grammatical roles or restructures meaning — not when it's multiple instances of the same subcategory.; examples: "The clock stopped{ at the when=>} before noon."; "Dinner and clean up {is well plannwill=>will} be very good and well digested, while I finish cleaning the kitchen."
  Morphological change (word family change affecting part of speech); examples: "The seam needs {strong=>strength to} hold a load."; "They asked {approve=>for approval} before moving the mural."; "The video is just a smooth pan moving left {slow=>slowly.}"
  Insertion or deletion of linking or modal verbs; examples: "Your timely response is appreciated, so {arrancan=>arrangements can} be made without further delay."; "The unit was well stocked , {Lights=>and the lights were} in working condition ."; "Are these {date are=>dates} the same?"
  Insertion or deletion of a conjunction; examples: "She looked at {then=>them and} asked, “What did you do?”"; "Please note {=>that the }shipment has been processed and is currently in transit."
  Pronoun insertion or deletion; examples: "If the latch is loose, tighten {with wrench=>it with a wrench}."; "The fern that {it grows beside shed=>grows beside the shed} needs more water."; "We finished the model, so we put {in the cabniet=>it in the cabinet}."
  Replacement of one functional word (preposition, particle, determiner, conjunction, linking or modal verb, pronoun) with another functional word (for cases that are not covered by any Trivial category); examples: "Did you find {you kes=>your keys} yet?"; "Since I knew some {off=>of them,} I joined the group for dinner."; "{During paint=>While the paint} was drying, we cleaned the brushes."; "You won't {loose none=>lose any} of your notes."
  Clarification: This applies only when the replacement involves a different type of functional word (e.g., determiner → pronoun, conjunction → determiner), or when the change does not fit within a single Trivial category (e.g., pronoun → pronoun). If a functional word is replaced with another functional word of the same type and that type can be found among Trivial categories (e.g., preposition → preposition, determiner → determiner), classify it under Compound_Trivial, provided the overall annotation meets the Trivial criteria.
  Three or more Trivial changes; example: "The clay {potts is oon table=>pots are on the table}."

  * Compound_Complex — Use when the annotation: Does not meet the criteria for Compound_Trivial or Compound_Moderate, and still falls under the core Other › Compound definition (i.e., a single correction covers multiple error types or does not belong to a specific category but is required for correctness).; examples: "Can you think of any prepositions that mean {same meaning=>the same thing?}"; "The volunteer {opened bridge the river=>lowered the bridge over the river}."

* Rephrasing: The main difference with the other categories is that the correctness of the sentence is not altered; the edit aims to improve clarity, formality, tone, or other stylistic aspects depending on the subcategory. Do not mix with formatting, as it is another standalone category.

  * Wordiness — description: marks cases where unnecessary, filler, or inflated phrases make a sentence longer without adding meaning. Avoiding redundant or verbose expressions makes writing clearer and more concise. Even if grammatically correct, shorter phrasing is often preferred for clarity and style.; examples: "Those videos {were helpful in teaching=>helped teach} me how to code."; "I spent {a two-week's time period=>two weeks} on this."; "{Discussion about feedback=>Feedback discussion}"; "The crew {made a decision=>decided} to repaint the backdrop."; "{In order to=>To} trace a circle, tie a pencil to a string."

  * Formality — description: used when informal words or phrases are replaced with more formal or professional alternatives.; examples: "The repair was {a piece of cake=>straightforward}."; "Please {give me a heads-up=>notify me} before closing the workshop."; "I'm {sorta=>quite} nervous."

  * Tone — description: marks a change that improves tone, making it more polite, confident, or appropriate for context.; examples: "I kept asking how was your health, but {you never replied to me=>I didn't get your reply} about that."; "{I think we should=>Let's} do it."; "We {need=>would appreciate} your help."; "{I guess this is related to this issue, do you=>Is this related to this issue}?"

  * Other — description: covers all other rephrasing changes made for clarity, excluding sentence splits.; examples: "A basket sits next to the coat rack. Put your gloves {in it=>inside the basket}."; "The teacher explained the {lesson=>topic} to the {students=>learners}."
